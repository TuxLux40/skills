# Regionale Besonderheiten / German-specific pitfalls

Things that make two records of one person look different, or two different
persons look alike. Check here before rating an identifier as conflict or
match. Regional rules have exceptions — confirm locally (GenWiki, parish
article) before building an argument on them.

## 1. Given names

- **Rufname.** Children received 2–4 baptismal names; the name used in daily
  life (Rufname) is often the *last* or second one, not the first. "Johann
  Peter Wilhelm" may appear later as Wilhelm, Peter, or Wilm. Johann/Johannes,
  Anna and Maria as first names are often mere prefixes shared by all siblings.
- **Johann ≠ Johannes** in some regions: Johannes as a standalone Rufname,
  Johann as prefix. Do not merge "Johannes" with "Johann Heinrich" on name.
- **Nachbenennung.** A deceased child's name was given to the next child of
  the same sex. Two baptisms, same name, same parents → look for the burial.
- **Naming after godparents and grandparents** produces same-named cousins.
- **Latin, dialect and short forms:** Joannes/Johann/Jan/Hans/Hennes;
  Henricus/Heinrich/Hinrich/Henrich/Hendrik; Guilielmus/Wilhelm/Wilm;
  Jacobus/Jakob/Köbes; Catharina/Trina/Tringen; Elisabeth/Elsgen/Lisette/
  Betha; Margaretha/Greta/Gretgen/Margret; Gertrud/Drütgen/Trautchen;
  Sibilla/Bilgen; Adelheid/Aleid/Alheit. Latin entries decline names
  (Joannis = genitive "of Johann", Annam = accusative) — not variants.
- French-period records translate names (Jean, Guillaume, Henri, Catherine).

## 2. Surnames

- **Spelling is fluid** until the civil registers enforce a form (and even
  then varies between offices). Treat variants as one name; record the spelling
  of each source verbatim.
- **Feminine endings:** -in/-s/-sche (Müllerin, Schmitzin, de Meyersche); the
  wife is normally recorded under her maiden name in Catholic books, often
  only as "uxor" or with first name in older Protestant ones.
- **Hofnamen / Genannt-Namen** (Westfalen, Lippe, Osnabrücker Land, parts of
  the Lower Rhine and Bergisches Land): the name belonged to the farm. A man
  marrying an heiress took the farm name ("Johann Schulte **genannt** Meier",
  gt., vulgo, modo, alias, sive). Children born before and after the move can
  carry different names; successive unrelated holders share one name. Prussia
  fixed surnames in the 19th century, sometimes as "X genannt Y". Track the
  **Hof** (Stätte, house number, Kolonat) as an identifier of its own.
- **Patronymics** in Ostfriesland (fixed surnames decreed 1811 under French
  rule, widely ignored for decades), Schleswig (fixed surnames by royal decree
  of 8 Nov 1771, in practice later), parts of the
  Lower Rhine: the "surname" changes each generation (Harm Janssen's son is
  Jan Harms). Fixed-surname adoption lists exist for the Napoleonic period.
- **Jewish families** adopted fixed surnames by state decree between c. 1787
  and the 1840s depending on territory (French decree of 20 July 1808 on the
  left bank; Prussian edict of 11 March 1812 for the then Prussian provinces,
  later rules for the others); adoption lists (Namensannahmelisten) link old
  and new.
- **Illegitimacy and legitimation:** child under mother's name, later under
  the (step)father's after marriage — look for the Randvermerk.
- **Translated and adapted names** on emigration (Schmidt→Smith, Müller→
  Miller, Schwarz→Black) and under Germanisation/Polonisation in the east.

## 3. Dates and calendars

- **Gregorian reform:** Catholic territories switched 1582–1584; Protestant
  German states only in 1700 (18 Feb followed by 1 Mar). Mixed-confession
  regions have double dating in between. A 10-day offset before 1700 between a
  Protestant and a Catholic record is not a conflict.
- **French Republican calendar** in French-administered areas until
  31 Dec 1805 (e.g. "12 Floréal an X"). Convert with a tool, cite both forms.
- **Feast-day dating** in older books ("Dominica Laetare", "am Tag Martini") —
  resolve via Grotefend, *Taschenbuch der Zeitrechnung*.
- **Month abbreviations:** 7bris, 8bris, 9bris, Xbris/10bris = September to
  December — not July to October.
- **Baptism vs. birth, burial vs. death:** older books record only the rite.
  Enter the event actually recorded (GEDCOM BAPM/CHR, BURI), never convert a
  baptism into a birth date.
- **Ages** at death/marriage are estimates; "majorenn/großjährig" depends on
  the law in force (commonly 25, later 21, in older law varying).

## 4. Places and territories

- Pre-1806 Germany was hundreds of territories; pre-1871 dozens of states.
  Jurisdiction decides which archive holds the non-church records. Identify
  the territory **at the date of the event** (GenWiki, GOV, HIS-Data).
- A village's **parish may lie in another territory or village**, and
  Catholics and Protestants of one village usually went to different parishes.
  After 1648 the minority confession often had to use the majority parish for
  registration — look in both.
- **Same place names** abound; always qualify (Kreis, GOV-ID). Places were
  renamed (esp. 1930s Germanisation in the east, post-1945 Polish/Russian/
  Czech names) and merged (Eingemeindungen; NRW municipal reform 1969–75).
- **Borders moved:** Alsace-Lorraine, Eupen-Malmedy, North Schleswig, Posen,
  West/East Prussia, Silesia, Pomerania, Sudetenland. Records may be in the
  successor state's archives, in German church archives, or lost.
- Write places in a fixed hierarchical form: locality, municipality/parish,
  district, state/province, country — historical form first, modern name and
  GOV-ID in a note.

## 5. Confession

A strong identifier. Record it for every person where the source shows it.
Mixed marriages: by custom or contract sons often followed the father's,
daughters the mother's confession; the wedding usually took place in the
bride's parish. Conversions were recorded. Reformed vs. Lutheran parishes
coexisted in many towns before the 19th-century unions (Prussia 1817).

## 6. Script and language

- **Kurrent** (to c. 1941) and **Sütterlin** (introduced in Prussian schools
  from 1915, dominant from the 1920s, dropped 1941) for German text; Latin
  script for Latin and often for names. Easy confusions: e/n/r, u/n (u has a
  bow), long s/f, h/y, B/L/C, K/R, St/Fl; double-m/n written with a stroke.
- **Latin in Catholic books** until c. 1800–1810; formulae and kinship terms
  in `glossar-de-en.md`.
- **Low German, Dutch (Lower Rhine, East Frisia), French (1798–1814),
  Polish/Latin/Russian** (eastern provinces; Russian obligatory in Congress
  Poland 1868–1917), Danish (North Schleswig).
- Transcribe letter-for-letter; expand abbreviations in brackets; never
  normalise names inside the transcription.

## 7. Occupations and status

Occupation/status terms discriminate between namesakes and are regionally
specific: Ackerer/Ackersmann (Rhineland farmer), Kötter/Kötner/Kossäth,
Brinksitzer, Heuerling (Westphalian tenant labourer), Halbwinner/Halfe (Lower
Rhine tenant farmer), Tagelöhner, Einlieger/Inste, Colon/Zeller (holder of a
full farm), Vollspänner/Halbspänner, Bürger (holder of citizenship rights),
Beisasse. Meanings and rank shift by region — look them up (GenWiki
"Berufsbezeichnungen") before equating two terms.

## 8. Biology and custom — plausibility bounds

Used by `scripts/gedcom_plausi.py` and for manual checks. These are flags for
review, not proof of error.

| Check | Flag when |
|-------|-----------|
| Mother's age at birth | < 15 or > 50 |
| Father's age at birth | < 15 or > 75 |
| Child after mother's death | any |
| Child after father's death | > 9 months |
| Sibling spacing | < 9 months (and not same day) |
| Age at marriage | < 14, or first marriage unusually early for the region (typical first marriage: mid/late 20s) |
| Lifespan | > 105 years |
| Event order | baptism before birth, burial before death, any event after burial |
| Remarriage | typically within months for a widower with small children — an early second marriage is normal, not a conflict |
